A watch estimates. It takes heart rate, age and a model built on someone else's blood, and calls the result your threshold. We take yours.

STEP 01

A ramp, not a race

The belt starts at an easy pace and climbs by 0.5 km/h every three minutes. You stop when talking stops, usually somewhere in the eighth stage.

40 minutes · treadmill
STEP 02

Six drops from a fingertip

At the end of each stage we take 0.3 µl of blood and read the lactate on the bench analyser. Six points is what a curve needs; four is a guess drawn through noise.

Lactate · mmol/l
STEP 03

The bend, found by fitting

Two thresholds come out of the fit: the aerobic one, where lactate first lifts off baseline, and the second, where clearance stops keeping up. Between them lives every easy run you will ever do.

LT1 & LT2
STEP 04

Zones on paper, in your watch by evening

You leave with five zones in pace and heart rate, a printed curve, and the same file exported for Garmin, Coros and Wahoo. Re-test after twelve weeks; the curve moves right when the training works.

Bench notes 04.08.2026

Why we stopped trusting the 20-minute test

A field test measures how well you pace a field test. Two runners with the same average power over twenty minutes came back with thresholds eleven watts apart on the bench — and the one who felt slower had the higher number.

Read the note
Method 19.07.2026

Six samples, and why not four

The bend is where the curve changes shape, so it needs points on both sides of it. Four samples put the fit at the mercy of one bad drop; six survive a smudged strip and a cold fingertip.
